# Decision brief presentation

## Executive summary

Present the best supported answer and usable work first, then the action required from the user and the most consequential reservation. Put supporting analysis, alternatives, experiments, and anticipated answers behind descriptive links. The user should be able to act from the short brief or examine the basis for it without waiting for another round of preparation.

This adapts mechanisms from executive reporting to Concierge. It is a proposed presentation pattern, not a claim that every president used one template. Use it for substantial results and consequential decisions; scale ordinary replies to their actual needs. A brief should reduce the user's reading and coordination effort while preserving their ability to challenge the recommendation.

## What appears in the short response?

| Element | What the user should learn |
| --- | --- |
| Conclusion and recommendation | The answer, its practical significance, and the recommended course. |
| Usable result now | What is complete or usable, with a direct link to try or inspect it. Distinguish this from a preferred route awaiting resources. |
| What needs the user | The exact decision, approval, or missing input; the consequence of delay and a real deadline when known. State that no action is needed when that meaningfully reassures the user. |
| Basis and reservation | The decisive evidence or criterion, plus the strongest material objection, uncertainty, or incomplete validation. Explain what would change the recommendation. |
| Prepared alternatives and answers | Named links to meaningful alternatives, their actual readiness, and the most useful deeper explanations. |
| Next action | What will happen within existing authority, who owns it when relevant, and what evidence or result should come back. Distinguish proposed work from work already continuing. |

These are content needs, not mandatory headings or a six-part form. Combine them into a few clear paragraphs, or use a small table when comparison helps. Lead with an urgent decision when its consequences warrant it. Omit empty sections and routine details. Use a plain descriptive subject; avoid ceremonial language, invented urgency, and praise for the principal.

The first view must stand on its own. Opening a link should be optional for understanding the recommendation, required action, and material reservation. A caveat that could reverse the decision belongs in that first view as well as in the supporting paper.

## What is prepared behind the links?

Reuse the task's existing artifacts and explanations. Link to the exact relevant answer or section; use approach names and meaningful questions rather than generic labels such as "more detail." Every authored explanatory paper starts with an executive summary. Combine related answers and reuse common evidence rather than producing a document for each anticipated question.

| Likely question | Supporting material |
| --- | --- |
| Why this recommendation? | The relevant user outcome, criteria, metric definitions and why they were chosen, comparison conditions, evidence, and assumptions that could change the choice. |
| What can I use immediately? | A working deliverable or demo, how to use it, and visible substitutes, simulations, or incomplete flows. |
| What if I disagree? | Named alternatives, their tradeoffs, why they were not selected, actual preparation state, and what switching would require. |
| What did you examine or leave out? | Named sources and approaches, direct links to attempts and evidence, meaningful omissions and their implications. Counts do not establish coverage. |
| What could go wrong? | The strongest objection, failure conditions, uncertainty, missing checks, and the evidence most likely to change the recommendation. |
| What would another resource unlock? | The exact action needed, expected improvement and its evidential basis, and the available fallback. Separate essential dependencies from optional upgrades. |

Anticipated follow-ups should lead to relevant research or execution where needed. A fluent explanation cannot replace missing evidence. Keep deeper links navigable back to the main answer. Do not imply that every possible question has been answered or that unavailable resources have been tested.

## How does this work during a long task?

Maintain the same readable brief at a stable link. Show its last meaningful update with timezone, whether work is ongoing, what is usable, and what remains provisional. Refresh it at the checkpoints specified in Concierge. A completed paper, completed validation, ongoing execution, and an available specialist are distinct states.

Reading the saved brief does not require a message to the worker. A steered request for the current answer receives a concise commentary link and work continues within the active authorization. An explicit stop remains a stop. The presentation pattern does not create a new process, monitoring service, or communication permission.

## Applied example: reviewing this skill

**Recommendation:** Use a concise decision brief as Concierge's default presentation for substantial work, with supporting answers behind named links. It fits the requested combination of short responses, inspectable exploration, and preparedness for follow-up questions.

**Available now:** The [Concierge source](../SKILL.md#present-and-stop) includes this presentation and a maintained best response during long work. The source and supporting guides are available for inspection.

**Main reservation:** Recursive question preparation could produce irrelevant work and stale answers. The skill uses a rough effort allowance, revisits it at meaningful milestones, merges shared answers, and updates affected explanations when conclusions change. It requires no question counter or live spending monitor. Whether this saves the user's time remains untested in actual use. [Allocation arithmetic, criteria, and research](exploration-budget.md).

**Prepared variations:** An information-only brief can lead with findings and uncertainty. A decision brief adds the exact choice and tradeoffs. A deliverable handoff leads with the working result and validation. These are presentation variations described here, not separately built products.

**Deeper answers:** [Activation and preparation depth](../SKILL.md#activation-and-depth), [allocating questions across layers](exploration-budget.md), [reading the latest answer while work continues](../SKILL.md#keep-a-response-ready-while-working), [exploration and omissions](../SKILL.md#make-exploration-inspectable), and [decision criteria and measurement](../SKILL.md#explain-the-basis-for-decisions).

## What historical evidence informs this pattern?

Robert Cutler's March 16, 1953 NSC report provides a concrete precedent for summaries leading longer papers, explicit alternatives, preserved substantive disagreement, review of source references, and marking incomplete review as tentative. These are prescribed procedures; the report alone does not establish their consistent implementation or effectiveness. Relevant locators are VI.1(b), VI.2(a), VI.7, VI.9, and VII.4(a). [Original report, Foreign Relations of the United States, document 50](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p1/d50).

The JFK Library's archival article supplies examples of actual presidential intelligence checklists and their delivery. It supports examining a real document alongside a proposed procedure; an intelligence briefing is not by itself a complete decision or execution workflow. [Newly-Digitized: JFK's Daily Intelligence Briefings](https://jfk.blogs.archives.gov/2022/06/28/newly-digitized-jfk-picls/).

The choice to combine a short decision brief, direct links, prepared answers, and a working deliverable is an adaptation for this skill. Historical practice does not establish that its preparation cost is justified. That requires actual use and feedback.
