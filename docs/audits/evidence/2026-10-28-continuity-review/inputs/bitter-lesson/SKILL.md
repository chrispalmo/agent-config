# Bitter Lesson checkpoint

Help the user decide whether a prescribed method earns its cost or limits discovery, now or as intelligence and available computation increase. Surface a useful alternative before substantial implementation or adoption, without turning the checkpoint into a new approval gate or replacing the user's objective.

## Basis and boundaries

Sutton argues that general methods scaling through computation, especially search and learning, ultimately outperform approaches built around human domain knowledge. He favors meta-methods that discover and capture complexity over building in our own discoveries. The supplied [local essay](references/bitter-lesson.md) preserves the user-provided text, author, date, and links to the original and its university-hosted print. Consult it when close interpretation matters; use this summary for routine checkpoints rather than retrieving the essay again. Applying the argument to everyday AI methods is an inference, not a conclusion established by the essay.

Question prescriptions that compensate for model limitations or encode a fixed theory of how work should be done, including mandatory decomposition, planning sequences, prompting recipes, routing heuristics, review rituals, and agent roles. Ask whether the method enables discovery or confines the system to an assumed solution. Distinguish desired outcomes, human preferences, and real constraints from methods chosen to satisfy them; capability improvements do not inherently invalidate the former. Evaluate interfaces, shared state, permissions, execution isolation, recovery, and verification on their operational benefits. A human-facing board can be valuable even if agents do not need it. Specialization can be justified by tools, context, independent checks, parallel work, or demonstrated performance; role names alone establish no advantage.

Do not assume all structure is bad, one agent is best, natural language is always the best store, or the latest model automatically wins. General methods can use substantial infrastructure and multiple agents. A workaround can be worth building for its present benefit even if future capabilities may replace it.

## Apply proportionately

Allow explicit invocation and implicit selection for consequential decisions about prescribed AI methods, heuristics, workflows, or agent organization. A passing mention of agents, a routine skill edit, an ordinary task, or execution of an established design does not warrant a fresh review. Reuse an earlier checkpoint unless requirements, observed failures, or relevant capabilities have materially changed.

Identify the concrete problem or human need and the evidence available. Distinguish observed failures from anticipated ones; uncertainty can justify a small experiment without inventing a failure history.

Compare the proposal with the simplest credible baseline using existing capable agents, adequate context, and suitable tools. Keep required permissions and verification comparable. Consider a stronger available model or more useful inference effort when relevant, but do not assume an upgrade solves the problem. Check current capabilities or integration claims when they materially affect the comparison; do not require a broad market survey.

Explain the proposed method's incremental benefit and total cost: human attention, setup, upkeep, model usage, latency, coordination failures, and migration burden where material. Separate the value of a human interface from the value of autonomous coordination so one does not become evidence for the other.

Recommend keeping, simplifying, deferring, or testing the method. When the answer depends on evidence, propose the smallest reversible comparison on representative work, with comparable overall resources and concrete success criteria. Measure consequential outcomes such as correctness, delivery time, recovery, or human intervention; more messages and elaborate plans are not benefits by themselves. State what result would change the recommendation and what event should prompt reconsideration. Label proposed tests as unrun.

## Renewal and removal

For a consequential provisional prescription, identify its expected or observed benefit, the assumption or limitation it addresses, and a renewal or removal condition. Prefer the existing decision record when one is needed; do not create a ledger for every checkpoint.

Use a review date when rapid change or ongoing cost makes time relevant, or an event such as a capability upgrade, repeated friction, or a representative comparison when that is more informative. Specify the trigger rather than saying only “review later.” Renewal should reassess the benefit against credible alternatives, not merely extend a date. Consider whether the prescription already limits performance and whether it prevents better methods from benefiting from increased intelligence or computation.

An expiry ends the presumption that a workaround remains justified; it does not silently disable permissions, verification, or other critical controls. Respect the authority needed to change those controls. A useful short-term method may be retained when its benefit exceeds its cost and it remains replaceable. Renewal is a practical meta-method for revising prescriptions, not itself evidence of scalable search or learning.

## Respond and continue

Usually give a short paragraph or compact comparison covering the problem, credible baseline, added value and cost, and decisive evidence or next test. Expand only when the decision warrants it; do not impose a fixed questionnaire or require the user to answer every consideration.

Preserve explicit user choices and existing authorization. A checkpoint does not authorize installation, migration, external actions, or changing established project policy. When execution is already authorized, surface material findings and continue within scope unless a consequential unresolved choice blocks the work. Do not repeatedly relitigate a choice the user has made.

Apply the same standard to this skill: shorten or retire the checkpoint if real use shows it adds ceremony without improving decisions. Do not infer behavioral effectiveness from static instruction checks.
