The desired outcome is **reliable continuation**: a successor can recover what the person wants, what constraints apply, what has actually happened, and what remains unresolved without access to the originating chat. My recommendation is to ask agents to maintain that outcome, while choosing the amount and form of preservation according to the work. An additional document or system is justified only when existing artifacts leave consequential gaps.

Judge any mechanism by recovery accuracy, respect for human authority, avoidable repeated work, and total cost—including human attention, agent usage, delay, maintenance, and migration. The useful amount of structure depends on the likelihood of interruption or transfer, the consequences of misunderstanding, how much existing artifacts already reveal, and the capabilities of whoever resumes.

My **prior-knowledge starting point** is that durable artifacts preserve different things. A repository can show implemented behavior; a research notebook can retain evidence; an operational log can show executed actions. None necessarily explains a private preference, an abandoned requirement, or why an apparently attractive option was rejected. My **deduction** is that some information must be deliberately preserved when it would otherwise disappear. Better reasoning can reconstruct implications from available evidence, but it cannot reliably recover an unrecorded human choice.

The supplied Bitter Lesson skill helps distinguish preserving information from prescribing a method of thought. Recording a consequential decision gives future agents something to reason about. Mandating a permanent planning sequence dictates how they must reason. Either can earn its cost, but the second needs a separate justification. Applying Sutton’s argument to work continuity is an inference, not empirical proof that sparse instructions—or elaborate infrastructure—will perform better.

A useful standing request would be:

> Leave consequential work recoverable from authorized, durable artifacts. Preserve information that a successor would otherwise have to guess or ask me to reconstruct. Keep actual state distinguishable from plans, and retain the authority and evidence behind consequential claims. Use existing artifacts where they suffice.

What counts as consequential is task dependent. The following are coverage questions, not mandatory fields for every task:

- **Intent and acceptance:** What outcome matters, what is outside scope, and what would establish completion? Preserve preferences that materially affect choices, including tradeoffs already settled by the person.
- **Authority and constraints:** What explicit permissions, prohibitions, and commitments govern the work? Preserve scope and relevant original wording where paraphrasing could change meaning. An agent’s summary cannot widen authorization.
- **Actual state and evidence:** What has changed, where are the outputs, what checks were performed, and what do those checks establish? Distinguish implemented, verified, proposed, and unknown.
- **Consequential decisions:** What was chosen, and what reason or evidence would prevent a successor from casually undoing it? Retain useful rejected alternatives when rediscovering their limitations would be expensive; do not preserve every exploratory thought.
- **Unfinished work and dependencies:** What remains open, what is blocked, and what external activity or commitment is outstanding? A suggested next action should remain identifiable as a suggestion unless the person has made it binding.

Start with the artifacts that already organize the task. A ticket might contain intent and decisions, a repository the implementation, and a test report the verification. An additional handoff may need only to identify those records and explain the gaps between them. If everything necessary is already discoverable and intelligible, no extra mechanism is needed. A separate summary that merely duplicates them adds another place to become stale.

Recoverability also requires practical access. A link into an unavailable harness does not solve continuity across harnesses. The successor needs a discoverable starting point and access to the relevant material. Sometimes ordinary files are sufficient; sometimes a database, notebook, or existing tracking system is more suitable. Where conflicting copies exist, identify which record governs the consequential fact. A polished summary should not silently outrank fresher evidence or explicit human instructions.

Maintenance should follow meaningful changes. A final handoff helps planned transfers, but cannot protect against an unexpected interruption. For consequential external actions, preserving whether an action was intended, attempted, confirmed, or left uncertain may prevent a harmful duplicate attempt. Concurrent contributors may need ownership boundaries, versioning, or conflict detection. These are responses to concrete coordination problems; the presence of several agents does not itself justify a hierarchy of roles or a large protocol.

Different work warrants different amounts of structure. A short, reversible edit with a clear request and an informative diff may need nothing further. A research project with changing hypotheses may need evidence provenance and explanations of uncertainty. An operation involving external commitments may need more precise execution state. A long writing project may chiefly need audience, editorial decisions, and unresolved factual questions. Human readability can independently justify a dashboard or brief, even when agents could reconstruct the same information without it.

My recommendation **assumes** agents can recognize consequential changes, update records accurately, and distinguish observations from inferences. If they cannot, an outcome-only instruction is insufficient. More explicit fields, constrained update procedures, or targeted checks may then be useful. A more capable agent might need fewer procedural instructions, reconstruct state more effectively, or make richer automatically captured evidence economical to use. It might also benefit from substantial indexing infrastructure. Capability does not imply that every form of structure should shrink. Whether a particular current system can perform these duties reliably would need later verification.

The **strongest alternative** is to avoid curated continuity records and reconstruct state on demand from durable primary artifacts plus automatically retained activity history. Relevant human instructions would also have to survive outside the originating conversation. This approach reduces ongoing summarization and preserves details that a summary might discard. It is especially attractive when resumption is rare, reconstruction is reliable, and storage and access are straightforward. It becomes less attractive when histories are noisy, decisive intent is missing, or recovery consumes substantial time and attention. I would choose it over maintained summaries whenever its total recovery performance and cost are better. A hybrid—retained evidence with a small, replaceable index—is also credible.

The main failure modes are substantial. Agents may confidently record an incorrect interpretation, omit an unusual but decisive constraint, or label unverified work complete. Summaries can become stale while retaining an appearance of authority. Concurrent updates can erase qualifications. Overpreservation can expose unnecessary sensitive material or burden every continuation with irrelevant history. Excessive structure can encourage agents to fill fields mechanically and inhibit approaches that do not fit the schema. Underpreservation can force the person to repeat decisions. These risks favor recoverable evidence, clear uncertainty, limited duplication, appropriate access, and revisable conventions; they do not establish a universally correct format.

There is **no supplied failure history or comparative evidence** here, so the recommendation is provisional. A small, unrun comparison could use representative work resumed without its originating chat: one condition using existing artifacts, another adding selective continuity maintenance, and, where feasible, another reconstructing from retained history. Keep permissions, verification requirements, and overall resource budgets comparable, including the cost of creating and maintaining records. Measure successful continuation, consequential misunderstandings, repeated work, recovery time, and human intervention—not document completeness alone. Include an interrupted action or conflicting update if those situations matter in practice.

I would favor less maintenance if reconstruction repeatedly performs as well at lower total cost. I would favor more explicit structure if selective notes repeatedly omit consequential information and the added structure measurably prevents that loss. I would reconsider the preservation strategy altogether if its records amplify mistakes more than they aid recovery. Representative failures, repeated maintenance friction, changes in collaboration patterns, or a material capability change should trigger review. Procedural workarounds should lose their presumption of necessity when their benefits disappear; human permissions and required controls remain authoritative.

**Work continuity** is a sufficient plain name. “Handoff” suits an individual transfer; “memory” is broader and can obscure whether a claim is authoritative, observed, or inferred. A new branded framework seems unnecessary.
