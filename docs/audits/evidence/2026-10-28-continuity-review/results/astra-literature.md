This structured review, current to **28 October 2026**, supports asking agents to preserve **consequential intent, evidence, and the current state of unfinished work in an accessible, durable location**. It does not support a universal documentation package, memory architecture, file length, or number of agents.

The strongest practical conclusion is conditional: preserve information that would otherwise require rediscovery or could change a successor’s decisions, then add guidance and coordination mechanisms where they demonstrably help. **Work continuity** is a suitable plain name for this capability.

Confidence is moderate in the general recommendation. Evidence is substantially weaker for the optimal implementation, especially across different harnesses, prolonged knowledge work, and the newest frontier models.

I searched public sources using combinations of *agent memory*, *task takeover*, *fresh-session resumption*, *handoff*, *instruction files*, *AGENTS.md*, *skills*, *reasoning effort*, *shared state*, and *coordination*, including searches restricted to 14–28 October. I followed relevant citations to original papers and opened methods, results, limitations, and version histories. Recent publication dates were distinguished from crawl dates and recirculated older findings. Revised experiments replace earlier versions here; retellings are not counted as corroboration.

This is a selective, primarily English-language structured review, without a registered protocol, exhaustive database search, or pooled effect estimate. No experiments were run. Model names below come from the underlying methods; harness names are distinguished from model identities. In-window evidence includes several useful new studies, but much directly relevant evidence still concerns earlier models.

The evidence supports separating four outcomes that are often conflated: solving a bounded task, remaining coherent during a long run, resuming in a new session, and coordinating overlapping contributors. A higher memory-recall score establishes none of the other three automatically.

For **actual resumption**, Handoff Debt supplies unusually direct evidence: a successor receives an interrupted repository and must continue editing it. Its principal benefit is reduced rediscovery, rather than a uniform large increase in success. Raw traces frequently outperform compressed notes, and ordinary summaries can match or exceed structured notes. Consequently, it supports preserving predecessor context, but does not establish a mandatory schema. Its excluded preparation cost also prevents interpreting the reported token reductions as total lifecycle savings. [Handoff Debt](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02875v2)

HSC-Bench strengthens the case for explicitly preserving commitments, updates and completed operations. However, Kimi K2’s average continuation score declines with structured state, despite an improved all-criteria pass rate. Retained completion records occasionally still trigger repeated operations. Full history remains stronger overall, while narrative summaries underperform structured state for the model receiving that additional comparison. Thus, successful preservation and successful use are separate problems. [HSC-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.36523v1)

For **memory architecture**, October’s evidence points in different directions for understandable reasons. MemoryTax favors capable agents searching an intact record over expensive advance extraction in its small recall setting. That is evidence against assuming elaborate memory is necessary; it is not evidence against durable storage, since the raw history file is itself durable context. [MemoryTax](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29823v1)

DSV-Mem instead exposes failures when facts are repeatedly revised and authority changes across dense artifacts. Its state-ledger intervention suggests value in preserving updates and their provenance. Together these studies motivate a distinction between *finding an old statement* and *determining which statement currently governs*. Neither establishes a universally superior vector database, graph, wiki or summary format. [DSV-Mem](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.08102v1)

For **instructions and skills**, the apparent contradictions also concern different interventions. Repository overviews may duplicate information agents can inspect; a version-specific API index or expert procedure may supply otherwise missing knowledge. This is a plausible explanation—not a directly randomized comparison across studies—for the difference between AGENTS.md’s largely null success effects and Vercel’s strong reported improvement. [Evaluating AGENTS.md](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3), [Vercel experiment](https://vercel.com/blog/agents-md-outperforms-skills-in-our-agent-evals)

SkillsBench provides broader positive evidence for curated procedural assistance, but its task and skill selection limits generalization to arbitrary work. Its module-count and length analyses do not establish that changing the same skill to a particular number of modules causes improvement. McMillan’s more direct structural manipulation finds no detected benefit within its narrow conditions. There is therefore no defensible universal line limit or “correct” file arrangement. [SkillsBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12670v4), [factorial study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10039)

**Model capability changes the useful intervention**, but not according to a simple “weaker needs more text” rule. Weak executors can fail to follow guidance already present; adaptation into actionable instructions can help. Stronger models may make earlier scaffolding redundant. Both observations support reassessing guidance when the model or task changes, rather than mechanically growing or shrinking it. [Skill Adaptation](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33024v1), [Anthropic’s harness account](https://www.anthropic.com/research/harness-design-long-running-apps)

Reasoning effort is another independent variable. More computation can improve aggregate results while increasing cost, and a successful expensive run does not reveal whether that expense was necessary. The evidence does not establish that high effort replaces durable context, or that low effort is generally adequate. Escalation after verified failure is relevant where reliable verification exists; its applicability to ambiguous writing or research quality remains uncertain. [Economics of Reasoning Effort](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.34491v1)

For **multiple contributors**, shared memory and coordination should be distinguished. Brinvik shows that shared correction notes can reduce repeated instruction across drafting roles, while also propagating unintended examples. Its agents work through a fixed task order; this does not test concurrent writes or conflict resolution. [Brinvik experiment](https://brinvik.com/en/journal/claude-managed-agents-shared-memory-ai-sales-agents)

Cursor’s account concerns those coordination difficulties more directly. Its failures caution against expecting a shared file and voluntary locking rules to resolve contention. The controlled scaling study separately shows that additional agents can help decomposable work and hurt sequential work. Neither supports requiring a coordinator, reviewer, or agent team for every task. [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents), [Scaling Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)

Durability can also preserve mistakes. Memory-poisoning experiments demonstrate an actual cross-session mechanism through which external text becomes a later behavioral instruction. This supports maintaining the distinction between human authority, observed evidence and agent inference. It does not prove that a provenance label alone prevents misuse, nor justify transferring the reported attack rates to current Claude or GPT models that were not tested. [Persistent Memory Poisoning](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13889v1)

My practical recommendation is an **outcome requirement with adjustable implementation**. The following is engineering judgment informed by the evidence, not an experimentally validated universal prompt:

> Keep consequential intent and unfinished work recoverable by someone starting without this conversation. Use the existing durable artifacts where practical. Preserve the goal, authoritative constraints and permissions, consequential decisions, verified current state, relevant evidence, uncertainties, and the next useful action. Update these when they materially change and before handing off. Distinguish observations, decisions and hypotheses; identify superseded information. Keep the record proportionate to the difficulty and consequences of resuming the work.

What deserves preservation depends on what a successor cannot reliably reconstruct:

- **Intent and authority:** desired outcome, acceptance criteria, explicit exclusions, permissions and their scope, and consequential human choices.
- **Decisions:** the selected approach and brief rationale where alternatives remain plausible; rejected approaches only when remembering them prevents costly repetition.
- **Work state:** completed and incomplete work, artifact locations or revisions, important checks and their results, failures, and outstanding operations.
- **Evidence and uncertainty:** sources, observation dates where freshness matters, assumptions, unresolved contradictions, and confidence where consequential.
- **Continuation:** the next useful step, blockers, required human decisions, and—when several contributors overlap—ownership and dependencies.

These are information categories, not mandatory documents or fields. For a small edit, the changed artifact and a short completion note may suffice. A research project may need an evidence table and unresolved questions; an operational workflow may need execution receipts; a writing project may need audience, editorial decisions and draft status. Existing issue trackers, documents, source control and task systems should usually be considered before creating parallel records.

A compact entry point can identify the authoritative artifacts and current task state. Supporting evidence can remain elsewhere and be retrieved when needed. If critical intent exists only in chat, preserve the relevant statement and its context in an authorized durable location. An exported transcript can be useful, but preserving every conversation is not a prerequisite.

For changing information, record which item supersedes which, rather than simply accumulating both. For completed external actions, distinguish *planned*, *attempted*, *confirmed* and *unknown*. A successor should verify consequential state against available receipts or systems before repeating an operation. These distinctions address failure modes in the evidence, although their precise implementation has not been comprehensively compared.

Keep upkeep primarily with the agents doing the work. Update at meaningful transitions: a changed constraint, settled decision, completed milestone, failed approach, or impending handoff. Higher interruption risk may justify more frequent checkpoints. Routine activity should not automatically create permanent guidance. Human review should concentrate on ambiguous intent, changed authority and consequential judgments, rather than mechanically maintaining every status line.

Choose additional structure according to the observed problem:

- If successors repeatedly reconstruct facts or decisions, improve durable state and discoverability.
- If they find the information but misuse it, improve clarity, applicability or verification.
- If a recurring procedure is unfamiliar, consider a targeted skill or reusable example.
- If contributors collide, add explicit ownership and reliable update mechanisms.
- If guidance consumes effort without improving useful outcomes, simplify or retire it.

Retirement needs care: “no improvement” can mean both configurations fail. Preserve the distinction between demonstrated unaided competence, ineffective guidance and an unresolved capability gap. Nor should a permission or constraint be removed merely because benchmark performance appears unchanged. [Beyond Gain](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.32322v1)

The appropriate evaluation criterion is **total useful-work cost and reliability**: outcome quality, successful continuation, repeated work, constraint violations, human corrections, agent tokens, latency, and preparation/maintenance effort. An optional future comparison should hold the task and starting artifacts fixed and vary one mechanism at a time. For continuity, it must actually start a fresh session with only the promised durable material. No such comparison was conducted for this review.

There are substantial remaining uncertainties. The literature does not adequately establish how well agents autonomously select and maintain consequential state over months, how often summaries silently distort permission scope, or how much human maintenance different arrangements require. Cross-harness portability is often asserted through file formats rather than demonstrated through repeated real task transfers. Evidence for research and writing continuity is thinner than for coding and recall.

I have **high confidence** in the logical requirement that otherwise unavailable intent must be preserved somewhere if it is to remain recoverable. I have **moderate confidence** that selective predecessor context and well-matched procedural guidance reduce some avoidable work. Confidence is **low** in any universal format, amount of detail, checkpoint cadence, automated memory product, or contributor count.

Evidence that would materially change this recommendation would include independent, repeated, multi-week comparisons on current frontier models, with actual interrupted work, cross-harness resumption, overlapping contributions, reliable outcome assessment, and complete accounting of human and agent overhead. Especially valuable would be demonstrations that simpler artifact-based recovery consistently matches richer state, or that a richer mechanism reliably repays its maintenance cost across varied work.
